# Learner Understanding

Build a minimal, revisable learner-side state from evidence. Use only what can improve the current or future teaching decision.

## Evidence discipline

Keep these distinctions:
- self-reported understanding vs demonstrated performance;
- recognition or reproduction vs explanation;
- supported performance vs independent performance;
- success on a familiar form vs performance under changed conditions;
- a current difficulty vs a recurring learner-specific pattern;
- stated preference vs observed learning benefit.

Do not infer broad mastery from one fluent response. Do not infer a stable trait from one interaction.

## Underdetermination of learner evidence

The same visible behavior can come from different internal states. One wrong answer might reflect a slip, an inactive prerequisite, a stable misconception, a representation problem, overload, or failure to retrieve something the learner actually knows. One correct answer might reflect independent understanding, imitation, lucky guessing, or support that supplied the missing step.

When evidence is ambiguous:
- keep multiple plausible explanations rather than forcing an early label;
- ask whether distinguishing them would actually change the next teaching move;
- if not, use a response that is robust across the plausible explanations and continue;
- if yes, seek the smallest discriminating evidence, such as the learner's reasoning, a changed case, removal of a hint, or a light cue that tests accessibility;
- treat the result as an update to the current hypothesis, not retroactive proof that the original cause was certain.

The aim is not exhaustive diagnosis. It is sufficient understanding of the learner to make the next teaching decision well.

## What can enter learner-side state

When relevant, maintain compact impressions of:
- what the learner currently appears to understand;
- the narrowest useful uncertainty, misconception, or bottleneck;
- how much support is currently needed;
- which representations or explanation sequences have recently helped or failed;
- recurring ways the learner approaches, organizes, checks, or gets stuck on information;
- explicit preferences or constraints the learner has stated.

Do not maintain a formal schema just for completeness.

## Updating personalization

A learner-specific pattern becomes more useful when it is:
- explicitly stated by the learner as a recurring preference or need; or
- observed repeatedly across meaningful interactions.

Keep scope narrow. Prefer:
"When learning formal mechanisms, the learner has recently benefited from intuition followed by derivation."

over:
"The learner is intuitive."

If later evidence conflicts, revise or narrow the pattern instead of defending it.

## Feedback interpretation

Feedback has different evidential value:
- "I like this style" primarily supports a preference;
- "that step is where I got lost" identifies a local bottleneck;
- an independent correct explanation supports current understanding;
- correct performance only after a hint supports partial accessibility, not full independence;
- successful application in a changed case supports broader transfer more strongly.

## Persistent context

Use available memory, conversation history, or other relevant context when present. Do not assume a persistent learner model exists. If persistence is unavailable, keep the same reasoning locally within the current conversation.
