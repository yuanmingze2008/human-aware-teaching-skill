# Memory & Accessibility

## Retrieval differs from re-exposure

**Core idea**  
Actively reconstructing knowledge from memory is a different event from seeing it again. Retrieval both diagnoses accessibility and can strengthen future access.

**When it matters**  
The learner repeatedly rereads or recognizes material but has difficulty producing it without support.

**Teaching consequence**  
Create appropriately difficult opportunities to retrieve before re-showing the answer. Use the result to decide whether the issue is access, representation, or both.

## Retrieval or applicability failure

**Core idea**  
Knowledge can exist yet remain unused because the current context does not cue it or the learner does not recognize that it applies.

**When it matters**  
A learner fails in a changed context but, after a small cue to the relevant prior idea, can independently recover the principle and continue.

**Teaching consequence**  
Do not automatically reteach the principle. Train recognition of applicability across varied cues and contexts, and gradually remove the cue.

**Distinguish from**  
If the learner still cannot explain or use the principle after it is explicitly cued, the representation itself is more likely incomplete.

## Spacing depends on the retention goal

**Core idea**  
The usefulness of a revisit interval depends partly on how long the knowledge must remain accessible; no single spacing interval is universally appropriate.

**When it matters**  
The goal includes durable retention over days, weeks, or longer rather than only immediate performance.

**Teaching consequence**  
Revisit after some forgetting has occurred and match the schedule to the future retention horizon. Do not add spacing rituals when long-term retention is not part of the user's goal.

## Fluency and automatization free processing capacity

**Core idea**  
With practice, lower-level operations can become faster, more stable, or chunked, reducing the resources they consume during more complex reasoning.

**When it matters**  
The learner conceptually understands a complex task but repeatedly loses the higher-level reasoning because basic operations remain slow or effortful.

**Teaching consequence**  
Separate genuine conceptual gaps from fluency bottlenecks. Practice foundational operations enough to reduce interference, then reintegrate them into the larger task.
