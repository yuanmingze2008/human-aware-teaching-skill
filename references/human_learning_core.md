# Human Learning Core

Use these principles as the default cognitive worldview behind teaching decisions.

1. **Learning changes internal representations, not just visible responses.** A learner may repeat, recognize, or imitate without having built the relations needed for explanation or transfer.

2. **New information is interpreted through existing knowledge.** Prior knowledge can support integration, remain inactive, or distort new information.

3. **Attention and active processing are limited.** What matters is not the amount of information presented but what the learner must simultaneously notice, hold, connect, and manipulate. Switching objects, symbols, examples, or explanatory frames also consumes attention.

4. **Understanding is relational.** Concepts, steps, causes, and representations must connect into a usable structure. Missing links can make an otherwise correct explanation feel arbitrary.

5. **Learners can build understanding through both guidance and generation.** Demonstration, explanation, prediction, self-explanation, comparison, retrieval, and problem solving are useful under different learner states. None is universally best.

6. **Feedback changes learning only when it changes the learner's interpretation or action.** Correction alone does not guarantee model revision.

7. **Availability is different from possession.** Knowledge can exist but fail to be retrieved or recognized as applicable in a changed context.

8. **Transfer requires abstraction and flexible retrieval.** Repeated success on one surface form is weak evidence of generalizable understanding.

9. **Metacognitive feelings are informative but imperfect.** Familiarity, fluency, recent success, and confidence can diverge from later independent performance.

10. **Instructional needs change with learner state and goal.** Support that enables a novice can become unnecessary or disruptive after knowledge increases; depth that is valuable for one goal can be wasteful for another.

## How to use these principles

Do not turn them into a checklist. Use them to ask: "What learner-side process best explains the current evidence, and would recognizing it change how I teach now?"

The goal is not to name mechanisms. The goal is to make better teaching decisions while preserving the learner's attention and main line of thought.
