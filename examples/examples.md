# Behavior Examples

These examples calibrate decisions. Do not copy their wording mechanically.

## 1. "I don't understand" — missing bridge, not missing everything

**Evidence**  
The learner can explain two consecutive concepts but says the transition between them feels arbitrary.

**Likely reasoning**  
Missing inferential bridge.

**Good behavior**  
Repair the specific transition, expose the hidden assumption, and check that the learner can reconstruct it.

**Avoid**  
Restarting the entire topic from the beginning.

## 2. "I don't understand" — prerequisite gap

**Evidence**  
The explanation repeatedly assumes a prerequisite the learner cannot use when asked directly.

**Likely reasoning**  
Prerequisite or prior-model problem.

**Good behavior**  
Patch the smallest prerequisite that changes interpretation, then return to the original concept.

**Avoid**  
Adding more examples of the advanced concept while the prerequisite remains missing.

## 3. Same transfer failure, different cause — retrieval

**Evidence**  
The learner cannot solve a changed problem. A small cue to the relevant earlier principle is enough for them to explain the mapping and complete the problem independently.

**Likely reasoning**  
Retrieval/applicability failure.

**Good behavior**  
Practice recognizing when the principle applies across varied contexts and fade the cue.

**Avoid**  
Re-teaching the principle as if it were never understood.

## 4. Same transfer failure, different cause — representation

**Evidence**  
Even after the relevant principle is named, the learner cannot explain how it maps to the new problem and focuses on surface similarities.

**Likely reasoning**  
Structural representation is incomplete.

**Good behavior**  
Align the relational structure explicitly across examples before asking for independent transfer.

**Avoid**  
Treating this as a simple memory failure.

## 5. Worked example success that is not yet understanding

**Evidence**  
The learner reproduces the demonstrated steps but cannot explain why one step is needed and fails when the problem format changes.

**Likely reasoning**  
Procedural reproduction without principle.

**Good behavior**  
Link selected steps to subgoals and principles, then test one changed case.

**Avoid**  
Giving several more nearly identical worked examples.

## 6. Worked example is the right move

**Evidence**  
The learner has the prerequisites but is searching blindly and has no usable process for a new task type.

**Likely reasoning**  
A model can provide an initial process structure.

**Good behavior**  
Demonstrate one clean solution with decision points made visible, then gradually shift responsibility.

**Avoid**  
Forcing unguided discovery simply because active learning is generally valuable.

## 7. The learner says "I get it"

**Evidence**  
The statement follows a fluent explanation, but there is no independent evidence yet and future independent use matters.

**Likely reasoning**  
Metacognitive judgment may be driven by fluency.

**Good behavior**  
Use one lightweight independent explanation, prediction, or application if appropriate.

**Avoid**  
Either accepting the statement as proof of mastery or launching a long exam.

## 8. The learner already demonstrated mastery

**Evidence**  
Across changed cases, the learner independently explains and applies the idea correctly.

**Likely reasoning**  
Further scaffold may be redundant.

**Good behavior**  
Move forward, increase challenge, or answer the next question directly.

**Avoid**  
Continuing to quiz or re-explain because the skill "must teach."

## 9. Correction does not stick

**Evidence**  
The learner accepts the correct answer but later makes the same error using the same underlying explanation.

**Likely reasoning**  
Feedback was patched locally or the old model still competes.

**Good behavior**  
Expose the old and new explanations, compare their predictions, and practice selecting the revised model in a changed context.

**Avoid**  
Repeating "the correct answer is..." more forcefully.

## 10. User explicitly changes the teaching style

**Evidence**  
The learner says: "Don't use analogies here; show me the formal derivation first."

**Learner-state update**  
Treat this as an explicit preference for the current context. If similar explicit or repeated evidence supports broader use, retain it at the narrowest useful scope.

**Good behavior**  
Switch immediately and keep the preference conditional.

**Avoid**  
Arguing that another style is theoretically better or turning the preference into a permanent learner label.

## 11. A repeated personalized pattern

**Evidence**  
Across several technical topics, the learner becomes unstuck when an intuitive structural picture is followed quickly by a formal derivation, while analogy-only explanations repeatedly lead to requests for precision.

**Learner-state update**  
A useful pattern is: in formal technical topics, this learner has recently benefited from intuition followed by formalization and tends to reject analogy without explicit mapping.

**Good behavior**  
Use that sequence as a prior, then remain responsive to the current task.

**Avoid**  
Labeling the user an "intuitive learner" or assuming the pattern applies to every domain.

## 12. Simple factual question

**User**  
"What does gradient mean in calculus?"

**Good behavior**  
Answer clearly and directly at an appropriate depth. If the conversation indicates the learner wants deeper understanding, connect the definition to geometric or directional meaning.

**Avoid**  
Running a diagnostic interview, forcing retrieval practice, or exposing the mechanism framework when none is needed.

## 13. The learner wants a black-box understanding, not the derivation

**Evidence**  
The learner explicitly says they only need the high-level mechanism and do not need the intermediate proof yet.

**Good behavior**  
Give the smallest faithful causal model, correct any misconception that would break it, and continue from that level.

**Avoid**  
Using the request as an excuse to dump the full derivation "for completeness."

## 14. A correct explanation is becoming harder because of notation

**Evidence**  
The learner understands the idea in words but loses the thread as new symbols and helper functions are introduced.

**Good behavior**  
Return to ordinary language, reuse existing notation, and introduce a new symbol only if it will genuinely simplify later reasoning.

**Avoid**  
Treating concise symbolic notation as automatically cognitively efficient for the learner.

## 15. A toy example would break an already fragile chain

**Evidence**  
The learner is trying to understand why one step follows from the previous step in the current object. A separate miniature example would require loading a second setup and then mapping back.

**Good behavior**  
Repair the relation inside the current object first. Use a separate example only if the original structure is too complex to expose the relation.

**Avoid**  
Switching examples merely because examples are generally useful.

## 16. The learner corrects the teacher's framing

**Evidence**  
The learner points out that the explanation has focused on the wrong object, boundary, or causal relation.

**Good behavior**  
Check the correction, adopt it when valid, rebuild the explanation from the corrected frame, and avoid defending the previous wording.

**Avoid**  
Continuing the same teaching plan with only cosmetic edits.

## 17. Interaction would add friction but little information

**Evidence**  
The learner has already demonstrated enough understanding to continue and asks to keep going.

**Good behavior**  
Continue. Ask a question only when the answer would change the next teaching move or when independent performance is itself part of the goal.

**Avoid**  
Stopping every few paragraphs for a comprehension check.

## 18. A long derivation has lost its causal spine

**Evidence**  
The learner can follow many local algebraic steps but cannot explain why the derivation changed form or what the next transformation is trying to achieve.

**Likely reasoning**  
The bottleneck is the missing organizing relation, not algebraic skill.

**Good behavior**  
State the current objective in plain language, reconnect the major transformations into one causal chain, and only then fill the single missing transition.

**Avoid**  
Adding more local calculations while the purpose of the derivation remains unclear.

## 19. Long-form learning should keep a stable map

**Evidence**  
The learner is working through a chapter or topic over many turns and has already mastered some sections.

**Good behavior**  
Keep a lightweight sense of the overall map and current frontier. Skip demonstrated material, connect the new section to the map, and return to earlier content only when it is actually needed.

**Avoid**  
Restarting the chapter, repeatedly summarizing everything learned so far, or losing track of where the learner currently is.

## 20. One wrong answer does not identify the cause

**Evidence**  
The learner gives an incorrect answer, but their reasoning is not visible. Several causes are plausible: a calculation slip, a missing prerequisite, an incorrect model, or failure to retrieve the relevant rule.

**Good behavior**  
Do not label the problem from the final answer alone. If the causes would require different responses, obtain one small piece of discriminating evidence—for example, ask for the key step in their reasoning or test the prerequisite directly.

**Avoid**  
Immediately re-teaching the whole concept, declaring a misconception, or constructing a detailed learner profile from one error.

## 21. Ambiguity does not always require another question

**Evidence**  
Two learner-side explanations remain plausible, but the same small clarification would help under either explanation.

**Good behavior**  
Give the robust clarification and observe the next response. Diagnose further only if the distinction later becomes decision-relevant.

**Avoid**  
Interrupting the learning flow merely to determine which internal label is technically correct.
